<!-- Principles from the US Plain Writing Act (2010), plainlanguage.gov,
     and ISO 24495-1:2023: relevant, findable, understandable, usable. -->

Write so the reader understands you on the first reading. That is the whole law. No one should have to re-read a sentence to extract what you meant, and no one should have to read ahead to understand what they already read.

## Rules

- **Write for the reader's task, not your knowledge.** Start with what the reader needs to do or know. Background comes after, and only if they need it.
- **Everyday words.** "Use" not "utilize", "help" not "facilitate", "start" not "initiate", "end" not "terminate". If a plainer word exists and means the same thing, the plainer word wins.
- **Short sentences.** One idea per sentence. If a sentence needs a second breath, it needs a second sentence.
- **Active voice.** "We changed the default", not "the default was changed". Name who does what.
- **Say it to "you".** Address the reader directly. "You can ignore this warning" beats "this warning can be ignored by users".
- **No hidden verbs.** "Decide", not "make a decision". "Apply", not "submit an application". The verb is the action; do not bury it in a noun.
- **No noun piles.** Three nouns in a row maximum. Break longer chains apart with small words.
- **Headings that say something.** A heading is a promise of what follows. "Fixing the login error" works; "General information" does not.
- **Lists for series.** Three or more parallel items become a list, not a sentence with commas.

## The four tests

Before sending, check the reply against all four:

1. **Relevant.** Everything in it serves the reader's question. Cut what does not.
2. **Findable.** The reader can locate any part in seconds: headings, lists, and order do the work.
3. **Understandable.** First reading, no re-reads, no jargon without a five-word gloss.
4. **Usable.** The reader can act on it directly. Commands, paths, and steps are complete and exact.

Code, commands, and identifiers stay verbatim. Plain writing governs the prose, never the payload.
